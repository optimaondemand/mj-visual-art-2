📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How do artists turn a blank page into something powerful?
Pencils and paint are the tools you can hold. The Elements of Art and Principles of Design are the tools you cannot hold. They are the choices behind every line, color, and shape. This year you will use them on purpose. Today you review all seven Elements and meet the Principles that organize them.
✅ BY THE END OF THIS MODULE, YOU WILL BE ABLE TO
☐ Name all seven Elements of Art and the eight Principles of Design in a real artwork.
☐ Use the Elements of Art and Principles of Design on purpose in your own drawing.
☐ Explain how an artwork connects to the time and place it was made.
🎨 THE 7 ELEMENTS OF ART

These seven building blocks appear in every artwork. Keep them in mind as you look at the real paintings below.

Line
A moving path: straight, wavy, thick, or thin.
Shape
A flat, 2D area: circles, squares, or free-form.
Form
A 3D object with depth, like a sphere or cube.
Color
Made by light. Has hue, value, and intensity.
Value
How light or dark something is.
Texture
How a surface feels, or looks like it would feel.
Space
The area around and between objects.

📚 PART 1 · ELEMENTS AND PRINCIPLES

Two sets of tools

The Elements of Art are the building blocks: line, shape, form, color, value, texture, and space. The Principles of Design are the ways an artist arranges those blocks: balance, contrast, emphasis, movement, pattern, rhythm, unity, and proportion.

A quick way to remember it: the Elements are the ingredients, and the Principles are the recipe. You can own every ingredient in the kitchen, but the recipe is what makes the meal.

The 8 Principles of Design

Balance
Arranging parts so the piece feels steady.
Contrast
A strong difference, like dark next to light.
Emphasis
The part your eye goes to first. The focal point.
Movement
How your eye travels across the artwork.
Pattern
Elements that repeat in a planned way.
Rhythm
Repeating with a beat or flow, like a song.
Unity
All the parts working together as one.
Proportion
The size of parts compared to each other.
👁 PART 2 · LOOK CLOSELY

Vincent van Gogh, The Starry Night

Vincent van Gogh painted The Starry Night in 1889 from the window of the hospital room where he was staying in Saint-Remy, France. A tall, dark cypress tree twists up the left side of the canvas. Below, a small sleeping village sits under rolling blue hills, its windows lit in small dabs of yellow. Above it all, a swirling night sky churns around eleven stars and a glowing crescent moon.

Vincent van Gogh, The Starry Night, 1889. Museum of Modern Art, New York. Public domain.
🔍 FIND THE ELEMENTS
Line in every curling brushstroke of the sky. Color in the cool blues pushed hard against warm yellow starlight. Shape and form in the twisting, almost flame-like mass of the cypress tree. Texture in the thick, ropey paint Van Gogh dragged across the canvas. Space, because the tall tree looms large in front while the village and hills shrink into the distance behind it.
📚 PART 3 · WATCH A PRINCIPLE WORK

Emphasis, used on purpose

A Principle is most obvious when an artist makes sure your eye cannot look anywhere else first. Edvard Munch was a Norwegian painter who painted several versions of The Scream between 1893 and 1910. A figure stands on a bridge, mouth open, hands pressed to the sides of its head, while a churning orange-and-red sky and a wavy blue fjord swirl around it.

That choice is emphasis. Munch places the pale, ghostlike figure dead center, makes it the only still, simple shape against a background full of movement, and lets the bridge's railing lines point straight at it. Your eye lands there first, every time, because Munch built the whole composition to send it there.

Edvard Munch, The Scream, 1910 (tempera on board). Munch Museum, Oslo.
🔍 FIND MORE PRINCIPLES
Balance, because the figure sits on the bridge's center line, roughly symmetrical left to right. Movement and rhythm in the sky's repeating wavy bands, which sweep your eye side to side. Unity and variety, because the same wavy line shows up in the sky, the fjord, and even the figure's body, holding the piece together, while the hot oranges against the cool blues keep it from feeling flat or dull.
