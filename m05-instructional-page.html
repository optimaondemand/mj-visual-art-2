📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How does an artist make a flat shape look like a solid object you could pick up?
The answer is value: the range from light to dark. When you control value with care, a flat circle becomes a round sphere, and a flat blob becomes a heavy pumpkin. In the 1600s A.D., Baroque artists pushed value to its limit. They lit one part of a scene brightly and let everything else fall into deep shadow. Today you study how they did it, then you start practicing it yourself.

👁 LOOK CLOSELY · THE FEATURED WORK
Caravaggio, The Calling of Saint Matthew, about 1599 to 1602 A.D. Public domain.

Caravaggio (about 1571 to 1610 A.D.) was an Italian painter who changed how artists used light. In The Calling of Saint Matthew, almost the whole room is dark. Then a single shaft of light cuts in from the upper right and lands on the faces and hands of the men at the table. Your eye goes straight to the light, exactly where the artist wants it.

🔍 WHAT THE LIGHT IS DOING
Value ranges from the bright lit faces to the near-black corners. Form appears because the light wraps around cheeks, hands, and folds of cloth, making them look round and solid. Contrast between the light beam and the dark room creates strong emphasis: the lit figures become the focal point.

📚 PART 1 · VALUE TURNS SHAPE INTO FORM

From flat shape to solid form

Value is how light or dark something is. Form is a three-dimensional object: a sphere, a cube, a pumpkin. Value is the tool that makes form look real on a flat page. A circle is just a shape. Shade it from light on one side to dark on the other, and your eye reads it as a round ball with weight.

Every shaded form has the same parts. Learn these four words now; you will use them in your sketch and your final pumpkins.

Highlight
The brightest spot, where light hits directly.
Midtone
The medium gray between light and shadow.
Shadow
The dark side, turned away from the light.
Cast shadow
The dark shape an object drops onto the surface near it.

A value scale is a strip that steps from white to black through grays. Artists draw one to train their hand to make smooth, even changes. This week's sketch trains that same hand control directly on a pumpkin, the form you will shade for real next week.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · A WORD FOR THIS LIGHT
Baroque artists had a word for the bold play of bright light against deep dark. Tap to see the term and what it means.
Chiaroscuro (key-ar-oh-SKOO-roh) means "light-dark" in Italian. It is the strong contrast between light and shadow used to model form. When the contrast is pushed to an extreme, with figures emerging from near-total darkness, artists call it tenebrism.
📚 PART 2 · VALUE ON ONE FORM

Caravaggio, Saint John the Baptist

Caravaggio, Saint John the Baptist, 1604 A.D. Public domain.

Look at the shoulder, the chest, and the bent leg. The light lands brightest on the parts that face it (the highlight), softens into a midtone as the body curves away, and falls into shadow on the far side. That smooth run of value is exactly what makes the figure look solid and rounded, not flat. This is the same skill you will use to make a pumpkin look heavy and real.

📚 PART 3 · CONTRAST AT THE EXTREME

Unidentified Flemish artist, The Taking of Christ (in the Garden of Gethsemane)

“The Taking of Christ” (in the Garden of Gethsemane), unidentified Flemish artist, in the style of Caravaggio, about 1620 A.D., Museum of Fine Arts, Boston. Public domain.

This painting shows Christ's arrest in the Garden of Gethsemane. We do not know the painter's name, but the style tells us a great deal: he was working in Caravaggio's manner, using the same bold light-against-dark that made Caravaggio famous. Here the contrast is pushed even harder. Most of the painting is deep black. A small light at the left edge catches a few faces and the shine on a soldier's armor, and the rest of the crowd is swallowed by shadow. This extreme version of chiaroscuro is called tenebrism. The strong contrast makes the lit faces feel tense and full of drama, which is exactly the feeling Baroque artists wanted. The painting now hangs in the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston.
