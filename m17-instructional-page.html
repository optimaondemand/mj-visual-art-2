Module 17
3D Mini Galleries: Designing a Space for Art
Instructional Page
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When you walk into a gallery, why do the artworks feel like they belong together?
All year you made flat artworks on paper. This last module turns the page on its side and into the third dimension. You become a curator: the person who decides which works go in a space and how they are arranged. A gallery is itself a kind of artwork. The choices a curator makes about form, space, balance, unity, and proportion are what make a room of separate pieces feel like one whole.

▶

▶

▶

🏠 FROM A FLAT PAGE TO A REAL SPACE

A drawing has length and width. It is two-dimensional, or 2D, which means flat. A sculpture and a gallery space also have depth, so they are three-dimensional, or 3D. A 3D object that takes up real space is called a form. You can walk around a form and see it from many sides.

When you design a gallery, you are working with two kinds of space at once: the positive space taken up by the artworks and props, and the negative space (the empty room) that lets a viewer move and breathe between them.

📚 PART 1 · THE CURATOR'S DESIGN TOOLS

Same words, new job

You already know these words from making single artworks. A curator uses the same Elements and Principles, but now the "artwork" is the whole room. Here is what each one means when you arrange a space.

Form
The 3D objects in the room: sculptures, frames, stands, the walls themselves.
Space
The room a viewer walks through, plus the gaps you leave between pieces.
Texture
The surfaces in the room, from a smooth frame to a rough props or fabric.
Balance
Spreading the visual weight so one wall does not feel heavier than another.
Unity
The sense that every piece belongs together, like one collection.
Proportion
How big each piece is next to its neighbors and next to the room.
👁 PART 2 · HOW A CURATOR ARRANGES A SPACE

Moving the viewer through the room

A curator thinks about a visitor's feet, not just their eyes. Where does a person enter? What do they see first? Where do they walk next? A good arrangement uses space to set a path, and uses balance so no corner feels crowded while another sits empty.

Unity is what makes the room feel like one show instead of a pile of random objects. Curators build unity by repeating something: a color, a frame style, a theme, or a subject. Proportion keeps the sizes sensible, so a tiny piece is not lost on a giant wall and a huge piece does not swallow its neighbors.

🔍 A CURATOR'S CHECKLIST
Before opening a show, a curator asks: Is there a clear first piece the eye lands on? Is the visual weight balanced across the walls? Does something repeat to create unity? Is there enough empty space to walk and look? Are the pieces in proportion to the room and to each other?
📜 FRAMES, LABELS, AND WALKING THE ROOM

Finishing touches that turn objects into an exhibit

Two more choices separate a pile of art from a real exhibit. The first is the frame: giving each piece an edge or mount tells a visitor where one work ends and the next begins. The second is the label. A real museum label is short: it names the title of the piece, the medium it is made from, and often a line about what the piece is doing. Without a label, even a strong piece can feel unfinished, because the visitor has no way to know what they are looking at.

Once everything is placed and labeled, a curator does one last thing: they walk the room like a first-time visitor. Which piece does the eye land on first? Does anything feel crowded or bare? Does something repeat enough to feel like unity, or does the room feel like scattered objects? A curator adjusts before calling a show finished, and so will you.

🎓 PART 3 · YOUR YEAR, IN ONE SPACE

The capstone

This is the last module of the year, so your gallery has a special job. You have made many artworks across these quarters. Now you get to be the curator of your own collection: a retrospective of the artworks you have made this year. You will design and build a 3D mini gallery and fill it with several of your own finished pieces from earlier modules, arranged and labeled on purpose.

This is what artists, designers, and curators do for a living. Choosing what to show and how to show it is its own creative act. By the end of this module you will have turned a year of single pieces into one exhibit that says, "Here is what I made, and here is how it fits together."
