Landscape: Hundertwasser-Inspired Landscapes
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How do artists make a flat page feel like it goes back for miles?
A landscape is a picture of an outdoor scene: hills, sky, water, buildings, or trees. The trick that makes a landscape feel deep is the way an artist stacks it in layers from front to back. Today you will learn those three layers, review how line, shape, and balance hold a scene together, and look at how one artist broke every rule about straight lines and dull colors.
🎯 I WILL BE ABLE TO
▶
Use foreground, middle ground, and background to make a flat picture feel deep.
▶

▶

🏔 THREE LAYERS OF DEPTH

Every landscape that feels deep is built in three layers. Near things look big, low on the page, sharp, and richly colored. Far things look small, high on the page, hazy, and washed out. Look at how each layer below gets lighter and softer the farther back it sits, and darker and sharper the closer it comes.

Foreground
The front. Lowest on the page, largest, darkest, and most detailed. The part closest to you.
Middle ground
The center. Medium size and medium tone, sitting between near and far. Often where the main action is.
Background
The far distance. Highest on the page, smallest, palest, and hazy. Hills, sky, or faraway shapes.
🎨 YOUR THREE TOOLS THIS WEEK
Line
A moving path. Wavy, curved lines feel alive and playful. Straight lines feel still and stiff.
Shape
A flat 2D area. Overlapping shapes, with the front one covering part of the back one, instantly creates depth.
Balance
Arranging the parts so the scene feels steady, not crowded on one side and empty on the other.

📚 PART 1 · DEPTH IN A REAL SCENE

Reading a landscape in layers

Look at a photograph of a green steel bridge crossing a built wetland, titled Water Pollution Control (Miller Hull, 1997). It is easy to read in three layers. Tall grasses fill the foreground, large and close. A curved stone wall and trees sit in the middle ground. The bridge towers and forested hills rise small and pale in the background. Your eye climbs from the grass at your feet up to the hills.

A painting can do the same with shape and color alone. In Formal Landscape (Martin, 2001), smooth orange and rust hills stack one behind another. A road and cars sit low in front, the rounded mounds build through the middle, and a pale sky closes the top. Because the front shapes overlap the ones behind them, the scene feels deep even though the surface is flat.

🔍 FIND THE DEPTH
Three quick signals tell you which layer is which: size (near is big, far is small), position (near is low, far is high), and overlap (whatever covers part of something else is in front of it).
👁 PART 2 · MEET THE ARTIST

Friedensreich Hundertwasser, HundertwasserHaus

Friedensreich Hundertwasser was an Austrian artist and designer who believed straight lines were boring and that nature has almost none of them. From 1983 to 1986 A.D. he designed an apartment building in Vienna, Austria, now called the HundertwasserHaus. It is the inspiration for your project this week.

In the building, the floors and walls roll in wavy, uneven lines instead of flat ones. The front is broken into blocks of bright, unexpected color: white, golden yellow, slate blue, dusty rose, and gray, all sitting side by side. Windows come in many shapes and sizes, and each one is outlined in its own color, with little triangle caps of red, blue, yellow, and orange over some of them. Bulbous towers, curling iron balconies, and small mosaic tiles add even more pattern, and real trees and ivy grow right out of the rooftops.

🎨 HIS SIGNATURE MOVES
Organic curves instead of straight edges. Bright, surprising colors placed next to each other. Pattern everywhere: stripes, dots, and shapes covering walls, windows, and roofs. You will borrow all three in your landscape.
