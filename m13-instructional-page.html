📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Where do all the colors come from, and how do artists keep track of them?
Out of just three colors, an artist can mix almost any other. The color wheel is the map that shows how. Today you learn the three primary colors, the secondary colors you mix from them, the full twelve-hue wheel, and the difference between warm and cool colors. Keep the wheel below in front of you the whole lesson.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS MODULE, YOU WILL BE ABLE TO
  • Name the three primary colors and the three secondary colors, and explain how each secondary is mixed
  • Place all twelve hues of the color wheel in their correct order, including primary, secondary, and tertiary colors
  • Sort colors into warm and cool families and describe how each family changes the feeling of an artwork

👁 A REAL ARTIST AND THE PRIMARIES

Piet Mondrian was a Dutch painter who built whole artworks out of almost nothing but the three primary colors. In Composition (no. III) blanc-jaune, also known as Composition with Red, Yellow, and Blue (1935/1942, oil on canvas, San Francisco Museum of Modern Art), a tall white panel is crossed by heavy black lines, and only a golden-yellow block in the upper right and red and blue blocks down the left margin break the white. He believed those simple, pure colors expressed a kind of order and balance.

📚 PART 1 · PRIMARY AND SECONDARY COLORS

The three you cannot mix

The primary colors are red, yellow, and blue. They are called primary because you cannot make them by mixing other paints together. Instead, you use them to build everything else. A long time ago, in 1666 A.D., a scientist named Isaac Newton used a glass prism to split white light into a rainbow, which showed that white light is made of many colors. Later, art teachers settled on the red, yellow, and blue system because it worked well for mixing paints.

Mix two primaries, get a secondary

A secondary color is made by mixing two primary colors:

Red + Yellow = Orange
Yellow + Blue = Green
Blue + Red = Violet

On the wheel above, each secondary sits exactly between the two primaries it comes from. The six in-between hues (red-orange, yellow-orange, and so on) are tertiary colors, made by mixing a primary with the secondary next to it.

📚 PART 2 · WARM AND COOL

Two halves of the wheel

Split the wheel down the middle and you get two families. Warm colors are the reds, oranges, and yellows. They remind people of fire and sunlight, and they tend to feel close and full of energy. Cool colors are the greens, blues, and violets. They remind people of water, sky, and shade, and they tend to feel calm and far away.

🔥 WARM
Red, red-orange, orange, yellow-orange, yellow. They seem to come forward.
🌊 COOL
Green, blue-green, blue, blue-violet, violet. They seem to settle back.

💪 BEFORE YOU MOVE ON

You now know where every color comes from. Three primaries make three secondaries, those fill out the twelve-hue wheel, and the wheel splits into warm and cool. Keep this map in mind every time you pick up a brush. Knowing it is what lets you mix any color you want on purpose.
