📚 PART 1 · WHAT IS SURREALISM?

Art from the world of dreams

Surrealism was an art movement that began in the early 1900s A.D. Surrealist artists explored dreams, the imagination, and the hidden parts of the mind. Instead of showing the world as it really is, they combined ordinary objects in unexpected ways to surprise the viewer and to challenge how we see things.

A few simple ideas show up again and again in Surrealist art:

Dreamlike
The scene feels familiar but strange, like a dream.
Unexpected pairings
Two ordinary things placed together that do not belong.
Ordinary, made strange
A common object shown in an impossible size or place.
Realistic style
Painted so carefully it looks real, which makes it more puzzling.

👁 PART 2 · MEET RENÉ MAGRITTE

The painter of calm mysteries

René Magritte was a Belgian artist born in 1898 A.D. He became one of the most important Surrealist painters. His work is calm, neat, and very realistic, yet also puzzling. He painted ordinary objects, such as clouds, apples, and hats, but placed them in situations that could never really happen. His goal was to make viewers slow down and question what they were seeing.

Look closely at the featured work below before you read about it. Magritte believed that what we see is not always what things truly are.

René Magritte, La condition humaine (The Human Condition), 1933 A.D.

In La condition humaine, a painting on an easel stands in front of a window. The painted canvas shows a tree, a green field, and a cloudy sky. Those painted parts line up exactly with the real view outside the window behind the easel, so you cannot tell where the painting ends and the real landscape begins. Magritte is asking a quiet question: is what we look at the real thing, or only a picture of it? The painting is now held by the National Gallery of Art in Washington, D.C.

🔍 WHAT MAKES IT SURREAL
Everything is painted realistically, yet the idea is impossible. A painting that perfectly hides the very view it is blocking is a calm, dreamlike puzzle. The ordinary parts (an easel, a window, a field) are made strange by how they fit together.

📚 PART 3 · TWO MORE WAYS TO MAKE THE ORDINARY STRANGE

Put something where it does not belong

In Time Transfixed (La durée poignardée), 1938 A.D., a small black steam train comes charging out of a fireplace, right where the flue should be, with a clean room and a clock on the mantel around it. Nothing is drawn in a weird way. The train and the room are both painted realistically. The strangeness comes from putting the train somewhere it could never be. The painting is now held by the Art Institute of Chicago.

View at the Art Institute of Chicago ↗
René Magritte, Time Transfixed (La durée poignardée), 1938 A.D., Art Institute of Chicago. Shown by link: this work is under copyright.

Change the size, or change the world around it

In Personal Values (Les valeurs personnelles), 1952 A.D., Magritte fills a bedroom with everyday grooming objects, but a comb stands taller than the bed and a giant wine glass towers over the rug. The walls are painted as open blue sky with clouds, as if the room had no edges. Small, familiar things become huge and dreamlike. The painting is now held by the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art (SFMOMA).

💡 THE MAGRITTE RECIPE
Take an ordinary object. Then change one rule about it: its size, its place, or the world around it. Paint it carefully so it looks real. That mix of believable and impossible is what makes a piece feel Surreal.
