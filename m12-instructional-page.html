📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How can three colors and a bold black line make a picture jump off the page?
In the 1950s and 1960s A.D., artists looked at comic books, ads, and magazines and decided that everyday pictures belonged in a gallery too. This was Pop Art. One of its most famous artists, Roy Lichtenstein, painted huge canvases that looked like comic panels. Today you study how he used the three primary colors, thick black outlines, and tiny dots, then you start your own digital pop art piece.

📚 PART 1 · THE THREE PRIMARY COLORS

Red, yellow, and blue

The primary colors are red, yellow, and blue. They are special because you cannot make them by mixing other colors together. Every other color comes from them, but they come from nothing else. That is why artists call them the starting point of the color wheel.

Yellow
Blue

Pop artists loved primary colors because they are bold, flat, and impossible to ignore. Used straight from the tube with no mixing, they look loud and clean, just like the ink in a printed comic.

👁 PART 2 · LOOK CLOSELY

Roy Lichtenstein, Whaam!

Roy Lichtenstein was an American Pop artist who made paintings that looked like blown-up comic-book panels. Whaam! (1963 A.D.), now held by Tate in London, is one of his most famous. It spreads across two panels. On the left, a fighter jet fires a rocket and a caption reads that the pilot pressed the fire control. On the right, the rocket strikes in a burst of red and yellow flame above the giant yellow word "WHAAM!"

Open the link below to view it, then look for three things at once.

🔍 FIND THREE THINGS
Primary colors: flat red and yellow with almost no blending. Bold black line: thick outlines around every shape, the plane, the flames, and the letters. Ben-Day dots: rows of tiny dots filling the sky, the printing trick Lichtenstein copied from real comics.

📚 PART 3 · THE COMIC-BOOK LOOK

Bold lines, dots, and sound words

Three tricks give Lichtenstein's work its comic-book punch. First, a thick black outline around every shape, so nothing looks blurry. Second, Ben-Day dots: small evenly spaced dots that printers once used to make a color look lighter or shaded. Lichtenstein painted them by hand and made them huge. Third, onomatopoeia, words that imitate a sound, such as BOOM, POW, BAM, ZAP, and SPLASH, often drawn in big bold letters to show action.

You can see all three in another famous piece, M-Maybe (1965 A.D.), now held by Museum Ludwig in Cologne: a blonde woman with a thought bubble, drawn in flat yellow, red, and blue, with bold outlines and rows of red dots across her skin.
