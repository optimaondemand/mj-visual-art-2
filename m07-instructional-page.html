📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How do artists make flat shapes on a page look round, heavy, and real?
The answer is observation. Realist artists draw what is actually in front of them, paying close attention to light, dark, and the true size of each object. Today you study a master of looking, Gustave Courbet, and you learn the four tools that turn a drawing into something solid: value, form, proportion, and emphasis.
🎯 I WILL BE ABLE TO
▶
Use value, the slide from light to dark, to make an object look round and solid instead of flat.
▶
Draw objects at true-to-life proportions so a still life reads as believable.
▶
Use emphasis to guide a viewer's eye to the most important part of a drawing.
▶
Describe how Realism showed the everyday lives of ordinary people, using Courbet's work as an example.
👁 START BY LOOKING
Gustave Courbet, Still Life with Apples, 1871 to 1872 A.D. Public domain.

Spend a full minute with this painting before you read on. Courbet did not invent these apples. He set real fruit on a table, looked hard, and painted exactly what the light showed him. Notice how each piece of fruit looks like you could pick it up and feel its weight.

🔍 WHAT THE LIGHT IS DOING
A single soft light comes from the upper left. Each apple is bright where the light lands and slips into shadow on the far side. That slide from light to dark is value, and it is what turns a flat circle into a round form you believe in.
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Look at Courbet's fruit again. Which part of it makes it feel the most real to you? (No wrong answer. Just check in with yourself.)
📚 PART 1 · WHAT IS REALISM?

Art that shows life as it really is

Realism began in France around the middle of the 1800s A.D. It grew up as a reaction against the dramatic, dreamy art of the time. Instead of myths, heroes, and perfect beauty, Realist artists chose to show the world as it actually looked, including the everyday lives of ordinary working people.

A few habits run through Realist work:

Accurate details
Objects and people are shown with careful, close attention.
True-to-life proportions
Things are the correct size and shape next to one another.
Natural colors and light
Colors and shadows match what the eye really sees.
Everyday subjects
Ordinary people, daily life, plain objects, and natural scenes.
👁 PART 2 · MEET GUSTAVE COURBET

The leader of the Realist movement

Gustave Courbet (1819 to 1877 A.D.) painted regular people doing regular things, without dressing them up or making them look grander than they were. That choice was bold for his time, and it made him the leader of Realism. Look at two of his most famous paintings of working life.

Gustave Courbet, The Stone Breakers, 1849 A.D. Public domain.

Two workers break rock by the road in plain, worn clothes. Courbet gives heavy, hard work the same serious attention other artists saved for kings. Strong value contrast, the bright shirt against the dark hill, makes the figures stand out.

Gustave Courbet, The Grain Sifters, 1855 A.D. Public domain.

A woman in red shakes grain through a sieve while others rest and work nearby. The bright red dress is the emphasis, the spot your eye goes first, set against the quiet browns around it.

📚 PART 3 · FOUR TOOLS FOR DRAWING WHAT YOU SEE

When you draw from observation, four tools do most of the work. You can find all four in Courbet's Still Life with Apples at the top of this page.

Value
How light or dark something is. A smooth slide from light to dark is what makes a surface look rounded.
Form
A 3D object with depth and weight, like a sphere instead of a flat circle. Value is how you build it.
Proportion
The size of each object compared to the others. Correct proportion is what makes a scene look true.
Emphasis
The focal point, the part your eye lands on first. Light and dark and placement create it.
🔍 SPACE COUNTS TOO
Notice the space in the still life: the basket sits a little behind the front apples, and the dark background opens up room around everything. Overlapping objects and a deep background help show near and far on a flat page.
